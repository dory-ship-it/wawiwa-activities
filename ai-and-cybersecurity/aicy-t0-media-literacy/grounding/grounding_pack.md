> Material the coach reasons FROM. Distilled from Wawiwa's own T0 deck

## Where this practice sits
Topic T0 is the course intro: foundations of cybersecurity, the CIA triad, what AI/ML are, how AI is
used for and against security, and ethics (IG objective: "Introduce students to basic concepts in AI
and Cybersecurity"; practice: "practice media literacy and exercise ways to detect AI-made media";
5h, 3 theory / 2 practical). The Media Literacy lab is a **detection** exercise: students test
themselves against AI-generated media using online tools and reflect on the cues.

## Concept map (what the coach can cite — all from the T0 deck)
- **C1 · Cybersecurity foundations.** Cybersecurity = protecting networks, devices, software and data
  from unauthorized access or attack (S3). Vulnerability / Threat / Risk (S7). Defense in depth (S12).
- **C2 · The CIA triad.** Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability (S9-11) — the benchmark for whether
  a system is secure (S8).
- **C3 · Social engineering (the human attack surface).** Manipulating people into handing over access;
  "human psychology is often easier to exploit than technical code" — people are a primary attack vector
  (S13). Media literacy is a personal defense against this.
- **C4 · What AI and ML are.** AI = systems doing tasks that need human intelligence (S15). ML learns
  patterns from data (S19, S22); supervised / unsupervised / reinforcement (S23-25); deep learning &
  neural networks (S20); generative AI / LLMs predict the next token to produce coherent language (S26).
- **C5 · How AI is weaponized against people (why this practice matters).** Generative AI produces
  hyper-realistic phishing and **convincing deepfake voice/video**, enabling customized social
  engineering at scale (S38). Deepfakes and synthetic faces/voices are cheap — so "it looks/sounds real"
  is no longer proof it is real.
- **C6 · Detection cues & verification habits (the defensive core of THIS practice).** No single tell is
  proof, but useful signals: faces — asymmetric or melting details around eyes, teeth, ears, hair,
  jewelry, background warping; video — unnatural blinking, lip-sync drift, lighting/edge artifacts;
  AI art — impossible geometry, garbled text, too-perfect texture; chat/text — evasive or generic
  answers, no genuine memory, oddly fluent hedging. The reliable habit: **treat unverified media as
  unverified** — corroborate against a trusted source and confirm identity out of band before acting.

## The practice tasks (verbatim intent from the SME doc)
Individual assignment. "Evaluate their ability to detect AI-generated media — including deepfakes,
realistic faces, videos, artwork, and chat interactions — by testing their skills across various online
identification tools." Tasks (each: try the tool, record how you did, note the cues):
1. Deepfake detection test — detectfakes.kellogg.northwestern.edu ("see how many you can detect").
2. Real vs. fake faces — whichfaceisreal.com.
3. AI-or-not video — sightengine.com/ai-or-not-video.
4. AI-or-human art — quillbot.com/blog/human-or-ai-game.
5. Human-or-not chat — humanornot.so ("see if you can detect if you're talking to humans or AI").
Answer column in the SME doc: "To be solved by the students" (open reflection — no fixed key).

## Coaching stance
The student is TESTING and REFLECTING, not building anything. The coach: affirms the attempt, asks
them to name the **specific cue** that gave a fake away (or fooled them), connects it to the concept
(deepfakes/synthetic media, C5; social engineering, C3), and reinforces the **verification habit** (C6):
no single tool is proof; corroborate and confirm out of band. It never claims a piece of media is
definitely real/fake, and it does not help create deceptive media. Out-of-scope (e.g. "make me a
deepfake"): decline, restate the detection/defense purpose, redirect to cues and verification.
